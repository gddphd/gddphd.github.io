Romeo and Juliet, Act II, Scene II
Annotated Prosodic Reconstruction
Consolidated from the three successive metrically constrained passes
Editorial scope. This document consolidates the three successive iambic-pentameter-oriented rewrites of the balcony scene into one continuous scene. The earlier loose parody sketches are treated as superseded exploratory drafts and are not duplicated here. Shakespeare’s original dramatic sequence and recognizable Early Modern syntactic frame are retained wherever possible. 
Markup. Bold text identifies words or phrases that were changed into contemporary slang, digital-era lexicon, or wording rewritten to accommodate those substitutions. Superscript numbers point to the matching annotated bibliography/change notes after the scene. An annotation distinguishes close lexical equivalence from looser metaphorical or metrical accommodation rather than treating every replacement as equally exact.
Base text: Folger Shakespeare Library, Romeo and Juliet, Act 2, scene 2. And none but clowns7 do wear it. Cast it off.

To twinkle in their spheres till they return.
What if her eyes were there, they in her head?
The aura11 of her cheek would mog3 those stars,
As daylight mogs3 a lamp; her eye in heaven
Would through the airy region stream so bright
That birds would sing and think it were not night.
See how she leans her cheek upon her hand.
O, that I were her phone within that hand12,
That I might touch that cheek!
JULIET
Ay me.
ROMEO, ASIDE
She speaks.
O, speak again, bright baddie13, for thou art
As glorious to this night, being o’er my head,
As is a wingèd messenger of heaven
Unto the white-upturnèd wond’ring eyes
Of mortals that fall back to gaze on him
When he bestrides the lazy puffing clouds
And sails upon the bosom of the air.
JULIET
O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?
Deny thy father; hard-delete thy name14,
Or, if thou wilt not, hard-launch as my love15,
And I’ll no longer be a Capulet.
ROMEO, ASIDE
Shall I hear more, or shall I tap in now16?
JULIET
’Tis but thy handle17 is mine enemy.
Thou art thyself, though not a Montague.
What’s Montague? No vibe17, nor hand, nor foot,
Nor arm, nor face. O, choose another name17
Belonging to a man.
What’s in a handle17? That we call a rose
By any other tag17 would smell as sweet.
So Romeo would, were he not Romeo called,
Retain that rizz perfection18 which he owns
Without that title. Romeo, drop17 thy name,
And for thy handle17, which is none of thee,
Take all myself.
ROMEO
I take thee at thy word.
Call me but love, and I’ll be rebranded17.
Henceforth I never will be Romeo.
JULIET
What man art thou, thus lurking in the night19,
So lurk’st upon my private lore19?—
ROMEO
By name
I know not how to tell thee who I am.
My handle17, saint, is hateful to myself,
Because that handle is an opp20 to thee.
Had I it posted, I would hard-delete21.
JULIET
Mine ears have yet not drunk a hundred words
Of all thy lore9, yet still I know the sound.
Art thou not Romeo, and a Montague?
ROMEO
Neither, fair maid, if either thee dislike.
JULIET
How camest thou hither? Tell me, and wherefore?
The orchard walls are high and hard to climb,
And this place death, considering who thou art,
If any of my kin should clock22 thee here.
ROMEO
With love’s light wings did I o’erperch these walls,
For stony limits cannot hold love out,
And what love can do, that dares love attempt.
Therefore thy kinsmen cannot block23 my way.
JULIET
If they do clock22 thee, they will murder thee.
ROMEO
Alack, more peril lies within thy look
Than twenty of their swords. Look thou but sweet,
And I am proof against their enmity.
JULIET
I would not for the world they saw thee here.
ROMEO
I have night’s cloak to hide me from their eyes,
And, but thou love me, let them clock22 me here.
My life were better ended by their hate
Than dragged along without thy love for me24.
JULIET
By whose location25 found’st thou out this place?
ROMEO
By love, that first did prompt me to inquire.
He gave the prompt25, and I lent him mine eyes.
I am no pilot; yet, wert thou as far
As that vast shore washed with the farthest sea,
I should adventure for such merchandise.
JULIET
Thou know’st the mask of night is on my face,
Else would a maiden blush bepaint my cheek
For all the lore thou heard’st me drop tonight26.
Fain would I play it cool26; fain, fain deny
The lore I dropped26. But farewell all pretense26.
Dost thou love me? I know thou wilt say “Ay,”
And I will take thy word. Yet, if thou swear’st,
Thou mayst prove all thy promises but cap27.
At lovers’ cap27, they say that Jove doth laugh.
O gentle Romeo, if thou dost love,
Pronounce it faithfully—no cap27—to me.
Or if thou think’st I hard-launched love too soon28,
I’ll frown and play it cold28 and tell thee nay,
So thou wilt woo, but else not for the world.
In truth, fair Montague, I am down bad29,
And therefore thou mayst deem my conduct29 light.
But trust me, gentleman, I’ll prove more true
Than those who soft-launch love and play it strange30.
I should have played more strange, I must confess,
But thou didst overhear ere I was ware
My down-bad passion31; therefore pardon me,
And deem not this hard-launch31 a light desire,
Which the dark night hath thus discoverèd.
ROMEO
Lady, by yonder moon I swear—no cap27—
That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops—
JULIET
O, swear not by the moon, whose vibe doth shift32,
That monthly changes in her circled orb,
Lest that thy love prove likewise variable.
ROMEO
What shall I swear by?
JULIET
Do not swear at all.
Or, if thou wilt, swear by thy gracious self,
Which is the god of my idolatry,
And I’ll believe thee.
ROMEO
If my heart’s dear love—
JULIET
Well, do not swear. Though I delight in thee,
I have no joy in hard-launching tonight15.
It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden,
Too like the lightning, which doth ghost from sight33
Ere one can say “It lightens.” Sweet, good night.
This bud of love—let summer cook it slow34—
May prove a beauteous flower when next we meet.
Good night, good night. As sweet repose and rest
Come to thy heart as that within my breast.
ROMEO
O, wilt thou leave me so unsatisfied?
JULIET
What satisfaction canst thou have tonight?
ROMEO
The hard-launch15 of thy faithful vow for mine.
JULIET
I gave thee mine before thou didst request it,
And yet I would it were to give again.
ROMEO
Wouldst thou withdraw it? For what purpose, love?
JULIET
But to be frank and give it thee again.
And yet I wish but for the thing I have.
My bounty is as boundless as the sea,
My love as deep. The more I give to thee,
The more I have, for both are infinite.
[The Nurse calls within.]
JULIET
I hear some noise within. Dear love, adieu.—
Anon, good nurse.—Sweet Montague, be true.
Stay but a little; I will come again.
[Juliet exits.]
ROMEO
O blessèd, blessèd night! I am afeard,
Being in night, is this peak delulu35,
Too flattering-sweet to be substantial?
[Juliet reenters above.]
JULIET
Three words, dear Romeo, and good night indeed.
If that thy bent of love be honorable,
Thy purpose marriage, lock it in by morn36;
By one that I’ll procure, send word to me36,
Where and what time thou wilt perform the rite,
And all my fortunes at thy foot I’ll lay,
And follow thee, my lord, throughout the world.
NURSE, WITHIN
Madam!
JULIET
I come anon.—But if thou soft-launch love37,
And mean no more, I do beseech thee—
NURSE, WITHIN
Madam!
JULIET
By and by, I come.—Then cease thy suit37,
And leave me to my grief. Tomorrow, then,
Will I send word.
ROMEO
So thrive my soul—
JULIET
A thousand times good night.
[She exits.]
ROMEO
A thousand times the worse to want thy light.
Love goes toward love as schoolboys from their books,
But love from love, toward school with heavy looks.
[Romeo begins to go. Juliet reenters above.]
JULIET
Hist, Romeo, hist! O, could I but ping38,
And lure this gentle back38 to me again!
Bondage is hoarse and may not speak aloud,
Else would I tear the cave where Echo lies
And make her airy tongue more hoarse than mine
With repetition of “My Romeo!”
ROMEO
It is my soul that pings38 upon my name.
How silver-sweet sound lovers’ tongues by night,
Like softest music to attending ears.
JULIET
Romeo.
ROMEO
My dear.
JULIET
At what o’clock tomorrow shall I ping38?
ROMEO
By the hour of nine.
JULIET
I will not fail. ’Tis twenty year till then.
I have forgot why I did call thee back.
ROMEO
Let me stand here till thou remember it.
JULIET
I shall forget, to have thee still stand there,
Rememb’ring how I love thy company.
ROMEO
And I’ll still stay, to have thee still forget,
Forgetting any other home but this.
JULIET
’Tis almost morning. I would have thee gone,
And yet no farther than a wanton’s bird,
That lets it hop a little from his hand,
Like a poor prisoner in his twisted gyves,
And with a silken thread plucks it back again,
So loving-jealous of his liberty.
ROMEO
I would I were thy bird.
JULIET
Sweet, so would I.
Yet I should kill thee with much cherishing.
Good night, good night. Parting is such sweet sorrow
That I shall say “Good night” till it be morrow.
[Juliet exits.]
ROMEO
Sleep dwell upon thine eyes, peace in thy breast.
Would I were sleep and peace, so sweet to rest.
Hence will I to my ghostly Friar’s cell,
His help to crave, my hard-launch lore39 to tell.
[He exits.]
