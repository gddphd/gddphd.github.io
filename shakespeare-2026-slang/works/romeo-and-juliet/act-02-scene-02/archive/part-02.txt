1. memes. Original: “jests.” The replacement makes Romeo’s opening contempt internet-native: a meme is an amusing or widely circulated online item, so the verbing of meme suggests turning another person’s wounds into shareable ridicule. This is a loose creative extension rather than a dictionary-established synonym for “jests.” Support: Folger base text; Merriam-Webster, “Meme” (updated 11 Mar. 2026).
2. cooks. Original: “breaks.” In the uploaded glossary, the cook-family of slang is used for brilliant creation or performance, while cooked marks defeat. “Window cooks” therefore recasts the light’s emergence as something spectacular being produced. Moderate semantic equivalence; particularly useful because cooks preserves the monosyllabic terminal stress. Support: Saltman glossary, pp. 22 and 30; Folger.
3. mog / mogs. Original functions include “kill” and “shame/outshine.” Current slang mog means to outclass or perform/look far better than someone else. That relation is especially apt where Juliet’s beauty surpasses the moon or stars; the substitution preserves the comparative rhetoric without preserving the literal violence of “kill.” Strong metaphorical equivalence. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Mog” (updated 5 Nov. 2025); Folger.
4. cooked. Original: “sick.” The glossary defines cooked as destroyed or thoroughly beaten and “he’s cooked” as done or defeated. Applied to the jealous moon, it intensifies Shakespeare’s image of a pale, grief-stricken rival. Moderate equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 22 and p. 30; Folger.
5. bestie / more fire. Original: “maid … far more fair.” Bestie is defined as a friendly address/close friend, while fire marks something excellent, exciting, or trending. “More fire” preserves the evaluative superiority of “more fair,” but bestie loosens the original mythological relation between Diana and her votary. Mixed strength: fire is a strong evaluative analogue; bestie is deliberately looser. Support: Saltman glossary, pp. 18 and 8; Folger.
6. drip. Original: “livery.” Drip is defined as fashionable style or outfit. Because livery is clothing and distinctive dress, this is one of the closest lexical substitutions in the project: both terms occupy the clothing/style semantic field. Strong equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 25; Folger.
7. clowns. Original: “fools.” The glossary’s clown behavior denotes foolish conduct that draws mockery. Substituting clowns retains insult, social ridicule, monosyllabic stress, and grammatical function. Strong equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 21; Folger.
8. queen / crush. Original: “lady … love.” Queen is used as a compliment to a woman’s excellence, while crush denotes infatuation. The pair separates Shakespeare’s social elevation of Juliet from Romeo’s romantic fixation. Moderate-to-strong functional equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 38 for queen; p. 6 for crush/infatuation; Folger.
9. lore / vibe / lore-dump. Original functions: “says nothing,” “what of that,” and “discourses.” Lore dump is defined as a large disclosure of backstory; vibe concerns perceived atmosphere/behavior. The rewritten cluster turns Romeo’s reading of Juliet’s silent eyes into contemporary interpretive language. Moderate metaphorical equivalence rather than literal synonymy. Support: Saltman glossary, pp. 32 and 49; Folger.
10. top-tier. Original: “fairest.” Top-tier is a modern evaluative compound meaning among the best/highest-ranked. It preserves the superlative ranking of the stars but loses the beauty-specific sense of “fair.” Moderate equivalence; primarily a lexical modernization.
11. aura. Original: “brightness.” Current slang aura denotes cool factor, star power, stylish swagger, or charismatic excellence. Because Romeo’s image treats Juliet’s cheek as radiating a superior presence, aura bridges literal radiance and social charisma. Strong metaphorical equivalence. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Aura” (updated 19 Dec. 2025 / current dictionary sense); Folger.
12. phone within that hand. Original: “a glove upon that hand.” This is not asserted as youth slang; it is a digital-era object substitution. A phone is a contemporary object habitually held against or within the hand, allowing Romeo’s wish to remain physically adjacent to Juliet while updating the prop. Loose accommodation; the erotic glove metaphor is not fully preserved. Support: Folger baseline.
13. baddie. Original: “angel.” Baddie is current slang for a confident, stylish, attractive woman. It preserves Romeo’s praise and visual admiration but deliberately replaces divine idealization with contemporary attractiveness/status language. Moderate functional equivalence. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Baddie” (updated 19 Dec. 2025); Folger.
14. hard-delete thy name / hard-delete. Original actions: “refuse thy name” and “tear the word.” The glossary defines hard delete as permanently removing content. In a digital identity frame, permanently deleting the offending name closely matches the dramatic desire to erase the Montague identifier. Strong digital analogue. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 29; Folger.
15. hard-launch / hard-launching. Original functions include “be but sworn my love,” “contract,” and the exchange of vows. The glossary defines hard launch as publicly revealing a relationship. Here it signifies moving from private feeling to explicit, committed relationship status. It is strongest where the scene concerns overt commitment; weaker where Shakespeare’s “contract” carries legal-marital overtones beyond publicity. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 29; Folger.
16. tap in. Original: “speak at this.” The glossary defines tap in as join or engage with. Romeo’s choice is whether to remain an overhearer or enter the interaction, making tap in a strong pragmatic analogue. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 46; Folger.
17. handle / tag / drop / rebranded / No vibe / choose another name. Original lexical field: name, word, doff, new baptized. Handle is established slang for a person’s name or social-media username; tag is a digital label; drop and rebrand recast renunciation and renaming in contemporary identity-management language. “No vibe” is a looser insertion that stresses Juliet’s claim that Montague is not an intrinsic bodily quality. Strong for handle; moderate or loose for the surrounding accommodation. Support: Dictionary.com, “Handle” (social-media username sense); Merriam-Webster, “Rebrand” (updated 10 Feb. 2026); Folger.
18. rizz perfection. Original: “dear perfection.” Rizz means romantic appeal or charm. It captures the specifically romantic attractiveness Juliet says Romeo would retain without the Montague name, but narrows Shakespeare’s broader “perfection.” Moderate equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 40; Merriam-Webster, “Rizz”; Folger.
19. thus lurking in the night / private lore. Original: “thus bescreened in night / so stumblest on my counsel.” The rewrite makes Romeo’s concealment “lurking” and Juliet’s private counsel “lore.” It preserves the asymmetry—he has overheard a disclosure not intended for him—while shifting the register toward online observation and personal backstory. Moderate accommodation. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 32 for lore; Folger.
20. opp. Original: “enemy.” Opp/ opps means opposition or opponents and has broadened beyond its hip-hop origin to general antagonists. Because the Montague name is literally Juliet’s familial enemy-marker, the substitution is semantically direct. Strong equivalence. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Opps” (updated 14 Aug. 2025); Folger.
21. posted … hard-delete. Original: “written … tear the word.” Posting and hard deletion form a digital analogue of inscription followed by destruction. The change preserves the action sequence—externalize the name, then erase it—while modernizing the medium. Strong functional analogue. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 29 for hard delete; Folger.
22. clock. Original verbs: “find” / “see.” Clock has a documented colloquial sense meaning notice, catch sight of, or realize. It therefore fits the risk that Juliet’s relatives will notice Romeo. Strong colloquial analogue, though not uniquely 2026 slang. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Clocked, Clocking In, and More New Senses of Clock” (2019); Folger.
23. block. Original: “are no stop to me.” Block preserves the literal obstacle sense while also evoking digital exclusion. The double applicability makes it a compact modernization without materially changing the proposition. Strong functional equivalence.
24. Than dragged along without thy love for me. Original: “Than death proroguèd, wanting of thy love.” This is an accommodation rather than slang substitution. It renders the same preference—immediate death over prolonged loveless life—in more transparent contemporary syntax while maintaining the dramatic antithesis. Support: Folger.
25. location / gave the prompt. Original: “direction” and “He lent me counsel.” Location modernizes the way a place is shared; prompt preserves the pre-existing verb “prompt” in the prior line and recasts counsel as an initiating instruction, with an additional contemporary computing/AI resonance. This is deliberate lexical accommodation, not a claim that location or prompt are youth slang. Support: Folger.
26. lore thou heard’st me drop / play it cool / lore I dropped / pretense. Original: Juliet says Romeo heard her speak; she wishes she could “dwell on form” and deny what she said, then abandons compliment. Lore drop names the involuntary disclosure; play it cool captures her wished-for conventional restraint; pretense modernizes the abandoned social formality. Moderate pragmatic equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 32 for lore dump; Folger.
27. cap / no cap / swear—no cap. Original semantic field: falsehood, perjury, faithful oath. The glossary defines no cap as not lying/being truthful. Thus cap can occupy the falsehood slot, while no cap reinforces Juliet’s demand for sincerity and Romeo’s oath. Strong functional equivalence, though the phrase is intentionally conspicuous in Early Modern syntax. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 34; Folger.
28. hard-launched love too soon / play it cold. Original: “too quickly won” and “be perverse.” Juliet fears that her feelings have become explicit too quickly and says she could affect resistance to make Romeo woo. Hard-launch encodes premature explicitness; play it cold encodes strategic emotional distance. Moderate-to-strong pragmatic equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 29 for hard launch; Folger.
29. down bad / conduct. Original: “too fond” and “havior light.” The glossary defines down bad as embarrassingly lovestruck or emotionally desperate. It closely maps Juliet’s admission that she appears excessively enamored. Conduct is a plain-English accommodation for havior, not slang. Strong for down bad; neutral modernization for conduct. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 25; Folger.
