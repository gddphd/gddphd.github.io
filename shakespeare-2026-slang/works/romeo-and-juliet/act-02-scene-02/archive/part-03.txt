30. soft-launch love and play it strange. Original: “more coying to be strange.” Soft launch is defined as subtly teasing something new. Paired with “play it strange,” it preserves Juliet’s contrast between overtly revealed love and deliberately indirect courtship. Moderate-to-strong social-signaling analogue. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 44; Folger.
31. down-bad passion / hard-launch a light desire. Original: “true-love passion” and “this yielding to light love.” These reuse established slang from notes 29 and 15 to maintain the scene’s internal lexical system. The first emphasizes lovestruck intensity; the second casts Juliet’s unexpectedly explicit yielding as a relationship reveal. Moderate equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, pp. 25 and 29; Folger.
32. vibe doth shift. Original: “th’ inconstant moon.” The glossary defines vibe shift as a notable change in mood or atmosphere. Juliet’s objection is precisely mutability: the moon changes, so it is a poor emblem of constancy. The Early Modern auxiliary “doth” lets the contemporary noun/verb phrase enter Shakespearean syntax without flattening it. Strong metaphorical equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 49; Folger.
33. ghost from sight. Original: lightning “doth cease to be.” Modern ghost as a verb means to cut off contact abruptly, and more broadly evokes sudden disappearance. In the lightning simile, “ghost from sight” preserves the vanishing action while importing the relational sense. Moderate metaphorical equivalence. Support: Merriam-Webster, “Ghost” (updated 11 Mar. 2026); Folger.
34. let summer cook it slow. Original: “by summer’s ripening breath.” The glossary’s let him cook means allow a plan/process to develop. Juliet’s bud metaphor likewise asks time and summer to develop love into a flower. The culinary metaphor is new, but the developmental logic is closely parallel. Strong functional metaphor, looser imagery. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 32; Folger.
35. peak delulu. Original: Romeo fears the night’s events may be “but a dream.” Delulu is current Internet slang for delusional or audaciously self-confident fantasy. “Peak delulu” therefore recasts Romeo’s fear that wish-fulfillment has outrun reality. Strong psychological analogue with deliberately comic register. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 24; Merriam-Webster, “Delulu” (updated 8 Jan. 2026); Folger.
36. lock it in by morn / send word to me. Original: “Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow / By one that I’ll procure to come to thee.” Lock in is defined as focus/commit fully; here it is used in the commitment sense demanded by marriage. The following syntax is rearranged so the messenger instruction remains after the new commitment phrase. Strong pragmatic equivalence with metrical accommodation. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 32; Folger.
37. soft-launch love / Then cease thy suit. Original: “if thou meanest not well … To cease thy strife.” Soft-launch is used here as the opposite of the committed marriage proposal: a relationship kept tentative or merely signaled. “Cease thy suit” is a syntactic accommodation that restores the courtship meaning after the slang insertion. Moderate equivalence. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 44; Folger.
38. ping / pings / tomorrow shall I ping / lure this gentle back. Original communicative machinery: Juliet wishes for a falconer’s voice to call Romeo back; Romeo says his soul calls upon his name; Juliet later asks when she should send to him. The glossary defines ping me as send me a message. Ping therefore compresses summons and later message transmission into a one-syllable modern communication verb. “Lure this gentle back” is the accompanying rewrite required after removing the falcon-specific noun. Strong communicative analogue. Support: Saltman glossary, p. 37; Folger.
39. hard-launch lore. Original closing phrase: “my dear hap to tell.” Romeo is going to the Friar to tell the new relationship’s extraordinary good fortune and seek help with marriage. Hard-launch lore combines the scene’s established relationship-reveal and disclosure vocabulary while preserving the closing cell/tell rhyme. Moderate semantic equivalence; strong internal-system and prosodic fit. Support: Saltman glossary, pp. 29 and 32; Folger.

Methodological Note
The annotations assess semantic fit rather than asserting that all slang meanings are fixed. The user-supplied glossary itself warns that social-media slang evolves quickly and that meaning depends on speaker, platform, and context. The adaptation therefore treats slang as context-sensitive lexical evidence, not as a stable one-to-one code. Prosodic judgments are likewise editorial: Shakespeare’s blank verse permits inversions, substitutions, elisions, feminine endings, shared lines, and other departures from a mechanically regular ten-syllable pattern.
