| D2. Baseline | Map the source line's stresses, rhetorical function, and significant formal features. | Source prosody map. |
| D3. Adversarial question | Ask what reconstruction would prove the term is not inherently incompatible. | Explicit disconfirmation criterion. |
| D4. Rebuild A | Test lexical relocation: first-foot inversion, post-caesura position, or another metrically licensed stress site. | Candidate and scansion. |
| D5. Rebuild B | Test full-syntax reconstruction, removing competing heavy stresses and rebuilding connective syntax. | Candidate and scansion. |
| D6. Performance test | Read both candidates at acting pace and assess natural pronunciation, rhetoric, register, and breath. | Performer-oriented result. |
| D7. Synthesis | Classify the problem as inherent lexical risk, placement/syntax failure, fixed-idiom gravity, or unresolved. | Finding with uncertainty. |
| D8. Audit | Record retained/rejected candidate and reason; never erase the failed version from revision provenance. | Audit entry. |

## 41. Heavy-Stress Placement Heuristic

Strongly trochaic or spondaic contemporary terms should be tested first at positions where Shakespearean blank verse naturally tolerates stress disruption.

- First-foot inversion: a strongly stressed opening can be theatrically natural and metrically licensed.

- Immediately after a caesura: the pause can reset the actor's stress architecture.

- After an unstressed connector/article: this can separate adjacent heavy stresses and reduce phonotactic collision.

- Avoid direct adjacency between two unrelated heavy compounds when the collision arrests rhetorical momentum.

Illustrative DRASP finding: the problematic cluster in 'go speedrun thunder...' was attributed not simply to 'speedrun' but to the adjacency SPEED-run THUN-. Repositioning or removing the competing stressed noun changed the result materially.

## 42. Lexical Rigidity and Colloquial-Gravity Classification

Prosodic feasibility depends partly on how much a contemporary expression may be altered without ceasing to be the expression. Revision C therefore adds a rigidity classification.

| Class | Description | Typical behavior | Editorial risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R1 - Flexible single lexeme | One word or compact compound whose placement can move freely. | speedrun, feral, cooked | Usually reconstructable through placement/syntax. |
| R2 - Flexible phrase | Multi-word expression whose syntax can tolerate limited inflection or relocation. | hard block, vibe shift, main quest | Moderate metrical risk; test stress clustering. |
| R3 - Fixed idiom | Recognizable phrase whose wording/order carries much of its cultural identity. | receipts or it didn't happen | High risk of prose pull and metrical distortion. |
| R4 - Terminally rigid expression | Expression with a highly specific phonetic ending placed at rhyme/closure position. | peak delulu in a required couplet ending | High rhyme and syntax constraint; terminal-position test required. |

A phrase may be perfectly intelligible and semantically apt yet fail because its colloquial gravity pulls an actor into contemporary prose. This is distinct from mathematical scansion.

## 43. Mathematical Scansion vs. Performed Verse Rule

A line that can be divided into five theoretical feet does not automatically qualify as successful verse.

The editor must distinguish:

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Metrical possibility | Can a plausible five-beat scansion be written on paper? |
| Natural pronunciation | Does that scansion match how a contemporary speaker actually stresses the words? |
| Register continuity | Does the line remain heightened dramatic verse rather than drop abruptly into prose or meme delivery? |
| Performance momentum | Can the actor deliver the line without robotic marking of feet? |
| Emotional function | Does the rhythm serve the character's emotional state rather than merely satisfy counting? |

The Othello DRASP test is the model case: 'receipts or it didn't happen' can be made mathematically scannable, yet residual performance risk remains high because the fixed idiom carries powerful conversational-prose gravity.

## 44. Rhyme Preservation and Sacrifice Gate

Rhymed Shakespearean material requires a separate formal test. Rhyme must not be silently discarded simply because a preferred slang expression is difficult to rhyme.

| Priority step | Required attempt | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RHY-1 | Identify what the original rhyme is doing: comic finality, prophecy, song structure, closure, mnemonic force, contrast, etc. | Rhyme function recorded. |
| RHY-2 | Test the preferred contemporary term at the original or equivalent terminal position. | Natural rhyme available or terminal-risk flagged. |
| RHY-3 | Test semantically equivalent same-tier slang/vernacular with more workable phonetics. | Alternative term candidate. |
| RHY-4 | Move the slang internally and place a compatible accommodation word at line end. | Lexical meaning preserved while rhyme function survives. |
| RHY-5 | Test slant rhyme, internal rhyme, consonance, or assonance if exact rhyme would distort syntax. | Partial formal preservation. |
| RHY-6 | Test full-syntax reconstruction without Yoda-like inversion, filler, archaic contamination, or infantile repetition. | Naturalness gate. |
| RHY-7 | If all natural reconstructions fail, permit rhyme sacrifice and activate formal compensation. | Loss documented; compensation required. |

### 44.1 Terminal-Position Risk

A fixed contemporary expression with an inflexible ending should not automatically occupy a rhyming line end. The 'peak delulu' DRASP test demonstrates that a terminal slang choice can force the preceding line into absurd or tonally incompatible wording merely to manufacture a rhyme.

### 44.2 Formal Compensation When Rhyme Is Sacrificed

When exact end rhyme is relinquished after the Rhyme Gate, preserve the original formal function through one or more compensatory devices:

- parallel syntax or repeated sentence frames;

- internal rhyme;

- assonance or consonance;

- alliteration;

- metrical/cadential closure;

- refrain or repeated lexical anchor;

- balanced antithesis;

- couplet-like semantic closure even without perfect phonetic rhyme.

The Nuance Loss Register must state that end rhyme was sacrificed and identify the compensatory form used.

## 45. Rhyme-Function Fidelity vs. Rhyme-Form Fidelity

Revision C distinguishes the exact phonetic form of rhyme from the dramatic function rhyme performs. Where perfect end rhyme cannot survive without severe semantic, syntactic, tonal, or prosodic distortion, the editor should preserve the function through another formal device rather than fabricate unnatural language.

| Fidelity type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Rhyme-form fidelity | Preserves the original end-rhyme or equivalent phonetic closure. |
| Rhyme-function fidelity | Preserves the dramatic/satirical/mnemonic/closing effect even if the exact phonetic mechanism changes. |

## 46. DRASP Boundary Rule for Fixed Multi-Word Idioms

R3 fixed idioms receive a heightened gate in G3-G5 material. The editor must test whether the phrase remains heightened dramatic speech after scansion rather than simply whether it can be counted into five beats.

If a fixed idiom produces strong prose pull, the editor should test, in order:

- a shorter member of the same lexical family;

- a semantically equivalent Tier B or Tier C expression;

- splitting the idiom across a syntactic turn only if cultural recognizability survives;

- retaining the concept but moving the exact idiom to annotation rather than dialogue;

- preserving the original Shakespearean line when modernization produces net dramatic loss.

## 47. Genre Claim Qualification

The DRASP reviews correctly identify a serious tragic-register risk, but Revision C does not adopt a strict comedy-versus-tragedy lexical bifurcation. Shakespeare's plays routinely mix comic and tragic modes.

Controlling rule: dramatic gravity is classified by beat, speech, and speaker—not by the genre label alone.

Therefore Tier A meme slang may be appropriate for a Fool inside a tragedy, while a sublime passage inside a comedy may require Tier C-D restraint.

## 48. Performer Variability and Residual Uncertainty

Even a successful paper reconstruction may be delivered differently by actors. Stress ambiguity must therefore be preserved as an uncertainty field when a term can plausibly shift from trochee to spondee or otherwise alter the intended beat structure.

- Record the intended stress pattern.

- Record plausible alternative actor stress.

- State whether the alternative materially harms the line.

- Prefer constructions robust to reasonable performer variation.
