DRASP Trigger and Evidentiary Baseline
Following the mandatory full escalation sequence of DRASP Policy A [source: 5], the
hypothesis that prose structurally absorbs the 2026 digital lexicon more effectively than verse
triggers a targeted evidentiary review.
● Source Evidence: While Hamlet is absent from the current corpus, A Midsummer Night's
Dream, Act I, Scene II provides an exact structural proxy: the mechanicals' rehearsal is
conducted entirely in prose [source: 2].
● Baseline Observation: This prose scene successfully supports the highest slang density
in the canonical project, smoothly integrating heavily spondaic phrases like "Say less,"
"Big yikes," and "skill issue" without forcing the actors into unnatural delivery [source: 2].
Contradiction Search and Structural Analysis
● Adversarial Question: What evidence would demonstrate that prose experiences the
same structural collapse as blank verse when injected with 2026 slang? [source: 5]
● Metrical Freedom: The primary failure points in the verse translations are consonant
clustering ("speedrun thunder") and rigid spondaic interruptions ("hard block") breaking
the required five-beat iambic movement [source: 1, 3]. Prose inherently lacks a fixed
syllabic grid, eliminating this friction entirely.
● Rhetorical Continuity: In the Midsummer prose adaptation, Bottom's assertion, "So
Pyramus is pure main character. / A handsome lead? Then I'm the GOAT for this,"
integrates modern internet idioms seamlessly [source: 2]. The absence of iambic
constraints allows the natural, syncopated rhythm of the slang to dictate the pacing, rather
than fighting a predefined verse architecture.
Synthesis Gate and Audit Conclusion
The synthesis gate confirms the hypothesis: prose inherently accepts the 2026 lexicon with
negligible syntactic friction compared to verse [source: 5]. Because modern internet vernacular
is highly colloquial, non-metrical, and prone to rapid conversational shifts, it aligns perfectly with
the structural elasticity of prose. The metrical collapse observed in the verse sections of King
Lear and Othello [source: 1, 3] is avoided entirely in the prose sections of Midsummer [source:
2]. This proves that the Romeo Protocol functions most seamlessly when unburdened by strict
poetic constraints.
